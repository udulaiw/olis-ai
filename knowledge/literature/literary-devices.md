# Literary devices

Naming a device earns little on its own. Every time, write: **Device → Evidence → Effect → Meaning → Relevance to the question**.
Example sentences on this page are invented by OLIS for illustration; they are not quotations from any text.

## Comparison: metaphor, simile, personification

- **Simile**: a comparison using "like" or "as". *Example sentence:* "Her voice was like broken glass." Effect: makes an abstract quality concrete and vivid.
- **Metaphor**: says one thing *is* another, with no "like" or "as". *Example sentence:* "The classroom was a zoo." Stronger than a simile because the comparison is stated as fact.
- **Personification**: gives human actions or feelings to something non-human. *Example sentence:* "The wind whispered through the trees." Effect: makes nature or objects seem alive, often to create mood.
- Sinhala classroom terms: simile = උපමා, metaphor = රූපක, figures of speech in general = අලංකාර.
- How to write about it: name the two things compared, say which qualities transfer from one to the other, and say what that suggests about the subject.

## Imagery, symbolism and motif

- **Imagery**: language that appeals to the senses (sight, sound, touch, taste, smell). Say which sense and what picture or feeling it creates.
- **Symbolism**: an object, colour, place or action that stands for a larger idea (a storm for inner turmoil). A symbol needs evidence from the text that it carries extra meaning; don't call everything a symbol.
- **Motif**: an image or idea that recurs through a work and builds meaning each time it returns.

## Irony and dramatic irony

- **Verbal irony**: saying the opposite of what is meant.
- **Situational irony**: the outcome is the opposite of what is expected.
- **Dramatic irony**: the audience or reader knows something a character does not. In drama, explain the effect on the audience: tension, sympathy, or dread.

## Sound devices

- **Alliteration**: repeated consonant sounds at the start of nearby words.
- **Assonance**: repeated vowel sounds in nearby words.
- **Onomatopoeia**: a word that imitates a sound ("buzz", "crash").
- **Rhyme** and **rhythm**: the pattern of sounds at line ends and the beat of stressed and unstressed syllables. A rhyme scheme is written with letters (ABAB).
- How to write about sound: say what the sound is like (harsh, soft, fast, slow) and why that suits the meaning. "The alliteration is effective" earns nothing; "the hard 'c' sounds make the attack sound violent" does.

## Exaggeration, contradiction and contrast

- **Hyperbole**: deliberate exaggeration for effect.
- **Oxymoron**: two contradictory words side by side ("bitter sweet").
- **Paradox**: a statement that seems contradictory but reveals a truth.
- **Contrast** and **juxtaposition**: placing opposites close together so each stands out more.
- **Euphemism**: a mild word used for something harsh or unpleasant.
- **Pun**: a play on words with more than one meaning.

## Repetition and structure in lines

- **Repetition**: repeating a word or phrase for emphasis.
- **Anaphora**: repetition at the *start* of successive lines or sentences.
- **Epiphora**: repetition at the *end* of successive lines or sentences.
- **Enjambment**: a sentence runs on past the end of a line without punctuation. Effect: pace, surprise, or a sense of something that can't be contained.
- **Caesura**: a pause in the middle of a line, usually marked by punctuation. Effect: hesitation, emphasis, or a break in thought.

## Tone and mood

- **Tone**: the writer's or speaker's attitude (bitter, nostalgic, ironic, tender). Name it precisely and prove it with word choices.
- **Mood**: the atmosphere the reader feels. Tone belongs to the speaker; mood belongs to the reader's experience.
- Tone can shift during a poem or passage. Find where it changes and why.

## Narrative devices

- **Foreshadowing**: hints of what will happen later.
- **Flashback**: a scene from an earlier time interrupting the present action.
- **Narrative perspective / point of view**: first person ("I") gives closeness but a limited, possibly unreliable view; third person can be limited to one character or omniscient (all-knowing).
- **Allegory**: a whole story whose characters and events stand for ideas (often moral or political).
- **Satire**: humour, irony or exaggeration used to criticise people or society.
