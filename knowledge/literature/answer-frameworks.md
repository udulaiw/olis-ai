# Literature answer frameworks

These are OLIS teaching frameworks, not an official marking scheme. They describe what good literature answers usually do.

## Command words in literature questions

- **Explain**: give reasons (what + why / how), not just description.
- **Describe**: give the relevant features or details accurately.
- **Analyse**: break the text into parts (words, techniques, structure) and show how each creates meaning.
- **Discuss**: explore several points and more than one interpretation, then reach a view.
- **Compare**: similarities AND differences, using the same criteria for both texts.
- **Evaluate**: make a judgement and support it with evidence; consider the other side.
- **How does the writer…**: focus on the writer's methods (language, structure, form) and their effect, not on retelling the story.
- **To what extent**: a balanced argument, then a conclusion that says how far you agree and why.
- **Comment on**: name the feature, then judge its effect or significance.
- Read the question twice: underline the command word, the focus (text, character, theme, technique) and any limit ("in this extract", "two techniques").

## Paragraph structures

- **PEAL**: Point → Evidence → Analysis → Link back to the question.
- **PETAL**: Point → Evidence → Technique → Analysis → Link.
- **Claim → Evidence → Technique → Effect → Interpretation**: useful for close analysis of a single quotation.
- Evidence is a short exact quotation (a few words is often best) or, without the text, a precise reference to the moment in the text. Never make up a quotation.
- Analyse single words: why *this* word and not a simpler one? What does it suggest?

## Literature essay structure

- **Introduction**: answer the question directly in the first sentence; state your argument (thesis); name the areas you will discuss. No long biography of the author.
- **Body paragraphs**: one main point each, using PETAL. Order points from strongest to weakest, or follow the text's structure.
- **Conclusion**: return to the argument, bring together the strongest points, give a final justified interpretation. No new points and no plot summary.
- Every paragraph must move the argument forward. Retelling the plot is the most common way to lose marks.

## Character analysis

- **Trait → Evidence → Explanation → Importance**: name the trait, prove it from the text, explain how the writer shows it, then say why it matters to the plot, theme or question.
- Cover motivation, relationships, conflict and change (or why the character doesn't change).

## Poetry analysis questions

- What does the poem say? (speaker, situation, what changes)
- How does the poet say it? (word choice, imagery, figurative language, sound, structure, tone)
- Why this way? What effect does it create?
- What interpretation can reasonably be made? Is there another reading?

## Reading comprehension

- **Literal** questions: the answer is stated in the passage; find it and point to the words.
- **Inferential** questions: the answer is suggested, not stated; give the evidence and explain the reasoning.
- **Vocabulary in context**: give the meaning the word has *in this passage*.
- **Writer's purpose and tone**: name the purpose or tone and prove it with words from the passage.
- **Summary**: your own words, main points only, no examples, no quotations, keep to the word limit.
- Never add information that is not in the passage.

## Fact, interpretation and opinion

- **Fact**: what the text actually says or does.
- **Interpretation**: a reasonable meaning drawn from the evidence ("this could suggest…").
- **Strong interpretation**: supported by several pieces of evidence.
- **Opinion**: a personal response; label it, and support it if you use it in an exam answer.
